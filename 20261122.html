The Bee, the Sheet, and the Long Wait for January

In July, BCC was eighth of 45 teams. After a fall of two titles, two semifinals and a map that kept getting bigger, it is fourth of 71. What changed at the top, what didn't, and what the fall says about the spring.

By the SOAR Analytics desk · Saturday, Nov. 21, 2026

BETHESDA, Md. — The Rally Bee was a bee. An actual bee, in a Gatorade bottle. It had no batting average, no pitch count and no line of its own on the new SOAR power rankings. Still, any account of the team sitting fourth on that sheet has to start with the bee, and with a Labor Day weekend. That weekend began with a 12–9 loss to Wild Bill on Saturday and an 8–7 loss to the Chantilly Chargers a few hours later, which left a team 0–2 in pool play and staring at a long Sunday. It went on to beat Snyder Bucks 7–4 and Prince William Cardinals 15–9 that Sunday. On Monday it beat the same Wild Bill club that had beaten it two days earlier, 6–3, to win the first tournament of the fall.

By the team's own accounting, the bee is what got them back. The bee itself did not last past Labor Day. Its spirit did.

Box scores have no column for that. They don't need one. Here is what the bee explains about this team: it does its best work after Saturday has gone wrong. Labor Day was part of a run that reached six straight tournament titles by late October. The second fall title came at the Sykesville wood-bat tournament, a weekend that opened in 47-degree cold and ended with a 10–3 win in the final over Elkridge. Elkridge had won the three previous meetings between the two teams. A team that keeps a lucky charm is admitting that it doesn't always know how a weekend will turn out. A team that keeps winning anyway has decided that it will find out.

Now it is the week before Thanksgiving. The bats are in garages, the bags are in the basement, and the Gatorade bottles have gone back to holding Gatorade. The team will not be together again until January. That is two months, and in 12-year-old time it is roughly the length of a geological era. Rogers Hornsby, asked once what he did all winter without baseball, said he stared out the window and waited for spring. This is a piece for everybody standing at that window.

Two sheets, four months apart

The first SOAR power rankings for this group came out on July 12. They covered 45 teams and 1,905 games from the fall of 2025 and the spring of 2026. BCC was eighth, 53–20–3 over the two seasons, and finished on a 9–0–1 run. Its six tournament titles were the most on the sheet.

The new sheet covers 71 teams and 3,894 games through Nov. 15, with this fall counting most. Every result is adjusted for the strength of the team on the other side. BCC is fourth. Our analytics rate it about four and three-quarter runs a game better than an average team on the sheet.

The top of the sheet barely moved. Loudoun Aces were first in July, and HCYP Blue is first now. Gambrills Athletic Club was third both times. The three best teams in July are the three best teams in November, in a slightly different order. Eight of July's top ten are still in the top ten. Thirty-nine of the 45 July teams are back, and 32 teams are new. BCC climbed four places while 26 more teams squeezed onto the page beneath it.

The neighborhood got bigger. The top floor didn't change hands.

They earned that floor. HCYP Blue went 25–3 this fall and beat its opponents by more than nine runs a game. Loudoun Aces went 25–6. Gambrills went 13–5–1 against the hardest schedule of any team in the top ten. None of them got there by finding soft places to play.

Six teams from the July sheet are not on this one: Renegades AC, the Sykesville Cyclones' Navy team, New Era, the Savage Eagles, LBC McManus-Ray and SoBo. That is not a verdict on any of them. The sheet can see only teams that cross paths with the clubs it follows and keep their games where the data can reach them, and teams at this age are held together by carpools, friendships and a coach's free Saturdays, any of which can change over a summer. Renegades AC, sixth in July, turned up exactly once this fall, beating Olney Henig 10–7 with nearly the same lineup, and some of those other boys are already turning up in other teams' box scores. A team that leaves the sheet has not necessarily left the game.

Fifty-five apiece

Here is the strangest number in BCC's fall. Against the other teams in the top ten, BCC played eight games and went 3–5. It scored 55 runs and allowed 55. Against everybody else it went 16–3 and outscored them 180 to 75.

The math makes it look worse than it was. Four of the five losses to the top ten came on two afternoons. Gambrills won a doubleheader in September, 10–3 and 8–5. HCYP Blue won one in October, 8–7 and 8–3. The three wins came against Wild Bill in the Labor Day final, against Olney Henig by 12–3, and against Elkridge in the wood-bat final. The gap between BCC and the very top is real, and it isn't wide. Our analytics put BCC about a run a game behind Loudoun Aces and Gambrills, and a little more than two behind HCYP Blue.

The way BCC closed even that much of the gap says a good deal about the team. Through the first of November its hitters had drawn 115 walks and struck out 113 times. More walks than strikeouts, at an age when a 2–0 count usually looks like an invitation. Twelve boys pitched. All four who caught also pitched, and between them they threw more than half the innings. Coach Rem doesn't coach in slogans. What he says in the dugout is about the inning in front of him, and then the inning is over and so is the lesson. The plan for the fall didn't need a slogan anyway. In a weekend tournament, the team with arms left on Sunday afternoon wins. Nobody was asked to carry the staff, because everybody was asked to help carry it.

Then came the two semifinals. At Winchester, on the last weekend of October, the Coppers ended the run of six straight titles, 11–9, one day after BCC had beaten them 11–1. The message out of the dugout afterward was short: We learned something about ourselves. Two weeks later, at the Ripken Experience in Aberdeen, BCC won both games on Saturday. On Sunday morning it beat the Delaware Stallions 6–2; the Stallions had the most dangerous lineup in that field and are 11th on the new sheet. Then it lost the semifinal to Blasters Baker, 5–4.

You win three straight. You lose by a run. The bracket does not care how close you came, and the drive home down I-95 is the same length either way. The verdict out of Aberdeen was not complicated: there is a lot of work to do this winter.

The window

The rankings are a measurement, not a verdict, and they will be out of date the first Saturday somebody throws a pitch in the spring. They can't see a growth spurt, a new changeup or a winter of tee work in a cold garage. They can't see what a team decides about itself between November and January.

That part belongs to the boys. They will be apart for two months, back together in January, and the work starts then. Out past the spring, in July, is Cooperstown. Nobody is talking about anything after that yet, which is exactly right.

The Rally Bee showed up when a team was 0–2 on a Saturday and out of ideas, and the team won the next three games. It was a bee in a Gatorade bottle, and nobody built it for a long career. It never needed one. What it left behind was a habit: a weekend isn't over after the first day, a season isn't over after the last game, and fourth isn't first, yet. Come July in Cooperstown, there will be Gatorade bottles all over the dugout. The bee won't be in any of them. It won't need to be.
