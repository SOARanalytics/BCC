Noah — Seth asked me to look at the whole year and tell you what the numbers see that the dugout might not. I'll give it to you straight, the way I'd want it given to me. First things first, though: fifty wins across two seasons, and the run differential got better in the spring while you were folding guest players into the lineup and bringing a kid back from an elbow. That's not a hot streak — that's the sustainability of the program showing up in the numbers. Your kids are bought in; the data says so as loudly as it says anything.

Run prevention — the glove side

You took away the running game, and it's the quietest big thing in this whole dataset. Opponents stole 5.0 bases a game off you in the fall. In the spring: 2.5. Cut in half. At 11U, where the steal is half the offense, that's worth a run or more a game — and it doesn't come free. That's catchers receiving and throwing, pitchers varying their looks, infielders covering. That's taught. Whatever you did behind the plate between November and March, keep doing it.

Your defense stopped giving away bases the other way too. Reached-on-error dropped about 30% season over season (1.4/g → 1.0/g). Add it up and here's the picture: the spring's run-prevention gains came almost entirely from the glove side and the catching position. Boring stuff, done fundamentally sound. That wins games you weren't supposed to win.

Offense

The offense got more dangerous without changing its approach. Here's the part I find genuinely interesting: your team's swing rate, whiff rate, strikeout rate, and walk rate are nearly identical fall to spring — the process didn't move an inch. But runs per game climbed from 8.4 to 9.1. Where'd it come from? Damage. 7 home runs in the fall, 21 in the spring. Same at-bats, harder contact. That's kids growing into their bodies and a hitting program that didn't panic and rebuild anybody's swing over the winter. If it ain't broke, don't fix it — and you didn't.

One thing to keep half an eye on: the lumber got a little more concentrated. Your top two bats carried 33% of total bases in the fall, 37% in the spring.

The soft middle

Your season has a soft middle. In the spring, innings 1 through 3 you outscored opponents by +124. Innings 4 and 6? You got outscored. The team that shows up in the first inning is a monster; the team that plays the fourth is beatable. My read: that's when your second and third arms come in and the first-time-through-the-order advantage runs out.

And the close games flipped. Fall: 8–2 in one-run games. Spring: 2–3–2. Now, I'll be honest with you — one-run records at this level are mostly coin flips, and 8–2 was never going to repeat. Don't lose sleep over it. But here's the encouraging part buried inside it: every close loss and tie came in April and May. In June you went 2–0 in one-run games. Your kids learned to close as the season went on. That's the trend that matters, not the record.

Pitching command

The staff's command went the wrong way while everything else went right. Walks allowed rose from about 12% of batters to 14%, strikeouts dipped, and first-pitch strike rate sat flat at 56% both seasons. So the defense got better, the catching got better — and the pitching gave the gains right back. Runs allowed per game: 5.71 fall, 5.73 spring. Dead even. Seth tells me first-pitch strike is the number that predicts everything at this age, and I believe him. That's your lever. Fifty-six percent is the number to move.

A usage note — just data, you make the call. Your most effective arm this spring (2.14 ERA, 12.1 K/9) threw the sixth-most innings on the staff at 16.1, while your most-used arms combined for ERAs north of 7 across nearly 600 pitches each. I know there are reasons the box score can't see — catching duties, arm care, Pitch Smart math, who's available on Sundays. Pitchers gave me a headache too, so I'm not telling you what to do. I'm telling you what the ledger says, and the ledger says the innings and the effectiveness aren't lined up right now.

Player development

Development at the bottom of the order is real. Wang went .179 → .306. Endo .153 → .278. Dickinson turned into a .500 / .635 leadoff terror who scored 43 runs in 30 games. You're building athletes, and the numbers on the kids who weren't stars yet is where a program proves it.

Two kids went backward, and they'll need arms around shoulders. DiPompeo went from 10 extra-base hits to 1, and Pressberg's bat dropped 80 points while his games played dipped — even as his pitching became the best on the staff (1.24 ERA, are you kidding me?). Both are usually a swing detail or a confidence detail, not a talent detail. Your eyes will know which.

If you only fix one thing

Simpler is better. If the dashboard surfaces three things, you pick one — so here's mine: build a real plan for innings 4 through 6. Who's the bridge arm, what's his first-pitch-strike job, and how do you keep the defense sharp when the game gets long. It attacks the soft middle, the close games, and the walk problem all with one decision. The rest can wait until fall.

The stolen bases cut in half and the .179 hitter turning into a .306 hitter — that's the stuff that tells me the kids in your program are getting better at baseball and staying in love with it. That's the whole job.

The season was a good one, Noah. Whatever you got to do today to get the job done — that's what you're going to do.
